Biomedical discovery has entered an era in which the limiting resource is no longer data, but our ability to integrate and interpret evidence. DeepEvidence, a new deep research agent, goes beyond retrieving facts and constructs explicit representations of scientific evidence.
Subjects
The scientific literature now grows by millions of publications each year, while biological knowledge is distributed across hundreds of specialized databases, ontologies and knowledge graphs. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have dramatically improved information retrieval and question answering; however, transforming fragmented evidence into coherent biological understanding remains fundamentally challenging. In a recent study in Nature Machine Intelligence, Wang et al.1 present DeepEvidence, an artificial intelligence (AI) system that shifts the emphasis from retrieving biomedical information towards conducting structured scientific investigation through systematic evidence exploration and synthesis. Instead of simply answering questions, the system attempts to build an explicit representation of scientific evidence as it reasons.
The emergence of LLMs has rapidly changed expectations of what AI can contribute to scientific research2,3. Early applications largely focused on summarization, literature mining or retrieval-augmented question answering. More recently, the field has moved towards agentic systems capable of planning, tool use and autonomous execution of research workflows. Systems such as Biomni4, ToolUniverse5 and OpenAI’s Deep Research6 have demonstrated that language models can coordinate searches, invoke specialized tools and synthesize information across several resources. At the same time, broader efforts such as AI Co-Scientist7, autonomous chemistry platforms3 and Virtual Lab8 have begun to explore how AI might participate in scientific discovery more directly.
Yet despite these advances, a persistent limitation remains. Most existing systems remain fundamentally answer- rather than process-centric. As a result, they perform shallow searches to retrieve relevant documents and invoke tools from a large search space, and do not acknowledge structural relationships within their evidence. This can naturally lead to issues with relevancy, coherence and specificity. Human scientific reasoning follows a different approach. Scientists rarely arrive at conclusions based on a handful of papers, particularly for non-trivial questions. They iteratively accumulate evidence, choose tools purposefully rather than exhaustively, reconcile conflicting observations, and gradually construct an internal mental model that evolves as new information becomes available. Scientific discovery is therefore less about finding individual facts than about organizing evidence into coherent explanatory frameworks.
DeepEvidence is notable because it explicitly attempts to model this process. Instead of treating literature retrieval as the end point of reasoning, Wang et al. propose a framework in which several specialized agents ‘collaborate’ to explore heterogeneous biomedical knowledge sources using complementary search strategies1. Breadth-first exploration rapidly surveys multiple knowledge resources to identify potentially relevant entities, whereas depth-first exploration recursively follows mechanistic and citation links to investigate promising directions in greater detail. Throughout this process, the system continuously constructs an evidence graph that records entities, relationships, observations and provenance, providing a structured memory of the investigation rather than relying entirely on the language model’s internal context window. It shows that informed, staged search beats undirected exploration in large heterogeneous knowledge bases. DeepEvidence’s plan–act–observe–replan loop is a direct attempt to mirror the scientific reasoning process. It maintains an explicit, revisable checklist that gets updated as each subagent reports its findings, rather than committing to an answer after a single retrieval pass (Fig. 1).
Although evidence graphs and knowledge graphs are well established within biomedical informatics, their role in the work by Wang et al.1 is different. Namely, the evidence graph functions as an evolving external representation of the research process itself. This distinction is subtle but important. Existing graph-based systems generally assume that knowledge has already been organized into a unified graph before reasoning begins. However, biomedical knowledge is fragmented across publications, pathway databases, protein annotations, disease ontologies, clinical trial repositories and numerous specialized resources that differ in structure, completeness and terminology. DeepEvidence attempts to construct a coherent evidence representation dynamically as the investigation proceeds.
The implications extend beyond biomedical literature search. The authors demonstrate applications across several stages of translational research, including target identification, mechanistic pathway reasoning, metabolic response prediction, clinical trial design and evidence-based medicine. These tasks share a common requirement: integrating heterogeneous evidence while maintaining mechanistic consistency and traceable provenance. The work suggests that successful biomedical AI systems may increasingly depend on how they organize and reason over evidence rather than simply on the size of the underlying language model.
This observation aligns with a broader shift occurring across scientific AI. The first generation of scientific foundation models demonstrated remarkable predictive capabilities, exemplified by AlphaFold9 and later AlphaMissense10, which transformed protein structure prediction and variant interpretation through specialized machine learning. More recent systems have focused on scientific reasoning itself. AI Co-Scientist7 explores collaborative hypothesis generation, Biomni4 provides general-purpose biomedical tool use, while Virtual Lab8 demonstrates autonomous iterative experimentation. DeepEvidence occupies an interesting position within this landscape. Rather than competing directly on predictive modelling or laboratory automation, it focuses on a different bottleneck: evidence synthesis. In doing so, it highlights that scientific reasoning may require capabilities distinct from both prediction and retrieval.
At the same time, important challenges remain before evidence-centric AI systems become routine components of biomedical research. One concerns the distinction between provenance and correctness. Recording where information originates does not necessarily guarantee that conclusions are valid. Biomedical literature is characterized by publication bias, conflicting experimental findings, varying methodological quality and context-dependent biological effects. DeepEvidence records these observations with explicit provenance, but ultimately relies on the underlying language model to reconcile contradictory evidence. Future systems may need explicit probabilistic reasoning, uncertainty quantification and evidence-weighting frameworks that distinguish high-confidence causal relationships from preliminary or conflicting observations.
A second challenge concerns evaluation itself. The benchmarks presented by Wang et al.1 are comprehensive and span many stages of biomedical discovery. Nevertheless, benchmark performance is unlikely to become the ultimate measure of scientific AI. The success of biomedical reasoning systems should arguably be evaluated by their ability to generate useful scientific discoveries, prioritize experimentally validated hypotheses, accelerate translational research or reduce the cost of experimentation. Future evaluations therefore need to include prospective assessments embedded within real research programmes, on subjects such as target prioritization and experimental testing. These evaluations should also consider practical measures of utility, such as token cost or time-to-completion, relative to manual human research.
A further frontier lies in multimodal scientific reasoning. The current implementation focuses primarily on literature and structured biomedical databases. However, much of modern biomedical research depends equally on imaging, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial biology, structural biology, pathology, proteomics and increasingly complex experimental datasets. Integrating these diverse modalities into coherent evidence representations represents an important opportunity for future research. Similarly, closer integration with laboratory automation and ingestion of experimental data could enable evidence-centric agents to not only interpret existing knowledge but also propose, execute and iteratively refine new experiments.
Perhaps the most interesting question raised by this work is not whether evidence graphs represent the optimal implementation, but whether scientific AI itself should be viewed less as a sophisticated chatbot and more as a computational research collaborator or a thought-partner. Human scientists spend relatively little time recalling isolated facts. Instead, they continually organize, challenge and refine evidence into evolving conceptual models. If AI systems are to participate meaningfully in scientific discovery, they may require analogous capabilities.
DeepEvidence therefore represents more than another biomedical agent. It illustrates a broader conceptual transition in scientific AI, from systems that retrieve information, towards systems that organize evidence in support of scientific reasoning. Whether evidence-centric architectures ultimately become the dominant paradigm with deep adoption within the biomedical domain remains to be seen. However, the work by Wang et al.1 suggests that the future of scientific AI may depend less on generating convincing answers than on constructing trustworthy representations of the evidence from which those answers arise.
