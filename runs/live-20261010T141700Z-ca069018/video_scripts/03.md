# Authorship After Automation Means Auditable Responsibility

## Narration

[Visual: A polished manuscript fades into the background. In front of it appears a chain: contribution, evidence, verification, disclosure, correction.]

When AI contributes to literature review, analysis, or prose, authorship cannot be reduced to the question, “Who typed each sentence?” A more demanding question is: can the named author reconstruct, verify, and defend the work?

That is the core of auditable responsibility.

MIT professor Sasha Rakhlin argues that, in a growing number of fields, a polished paper is becoming a weaker signal of individual expertise. He proposes that evaluation should establish what a researcher contributed and accepts intellectual responsibility for, including when AI performed substantial parts of the work. [claim:03-c1]

[Visual: Contribution statement with fields for question formulation, data, analysis, verification, writing assistance, and final approval.]

For a researcher, that means being able to identify which ideas and decisions were yours; which tasks were delegated; which outputs were accepted, changed, or rejected; and why. It also means retaining enough evidence to reproduce central results and explain methodological choices.

Process records can help. Rakhlin suggests that tracing research workflows could preserve the lineage of ideas and make contributions—including those of graduate students—easier to recognize. But a record is not proof of understanding, and provenance is not correctness.

A *Nature Machine Intelligence* commentary makes that distinction explicit: recording where information originated does not necessarily make a conclusion valid. The literature itself may contain bias, conflicting results, uneven methods, or context-dependent effects. [claim:03-c2]

[Visual: A source card receives a “Provenance recorded” checkmark while “Claim validated” remains unchecked.]

So an auditable workflow needs more than prompt logs. For each important claim, preserve the cited source, the exact supporting passage or result, any relevant transformation or computation, and the dependencies connecting it to the conclusion. Then verify that chain independently.

A reviewer speaking in a commercial SciSpace webinar describes acceptable assistance in terms of continuity of intellectual ownership: it remains the researcher’s work, idea, and collected data. [claim:03-c3] That is a practitioner perspective presented by a company selling research tools, not independent validation of those products. Still, it identifies an important boundary: editing assistance does not transfer responsibility away from the researcher.

[Visual: The researcher remains at the center while tools assist with outlining, language revision, and evidence organization.]

Periodic Labs’ founders offer a more expansive view of process lineage. On the *Latent Space* podcast, they describe capturing conversations, intuitions, laboratory actions, computations, and code so that models can learn from the process of science rather than only its final outputs. [claim:03-c4]

That description is commercially interested founder testimony. It also exposes a tradeoff. Detailed lineage may support accountability and training, but it can burden researchers, reveal confidential ideas or data, and create surveillance-like records. The SciSpace webinar separately advises researchers to think carefully about data and privacy before sharing material with any AI system.

[Visual: A record-retention control with three labels: necessary, sensitive, unnecessary. No real laboratory data shown.]

The goal, then, is not to save everything. It is to keep proportionate records that allow consequential claims and contributions to be audited without exposing material that should remain private.

Correction is part of authorship too. Retraction Watch reported a case in which an error in one AI-generated mathematics manuscript affected two dependent papers. OpenAI’s stated response was to correct errors promptly and withdraw papers when no fix could be found. Responsibility therefore extends beyond initial release to dependent outputs, revisions, and withdrawal decisions.

Across the sources, responsible authorship is best understood as an auditable chain of contribution, evidence, verification, disclosure, and correction—not ownership of polished final prose alone. [claim:03-c5]

[Visual: Final checklist. “Can I explain my contribution? Trace the evidence? Reproduce the result? Verify the citations? Disclose material AI use? Correct the record?”]

No universal logging threshold will fit every discipline. Documentation can be excessive, and it cannot prove genuine understanding. But the human author should be able to answer those six questions—and accept responsibility when the chain fails.
