# Use AI to Widen the Search, Not to Choose the Question

## Narration

[Visual: A researcher’s question at the center of a blank canvas. Candidate papers, concepts, and methods gradually appear around it as labeled cards.]

When an AI system proposes twenty dissertation topics in thirty seconds, it can feel as if topic selection has become easy. But generating possible directions is not the same as identifying a research question that is original, consequential, and defensible.

The useful role for AI is narrower: widen and organize the search. The researcher must still choose the question.

[Visual: Two-stage diagram: “Explore broadly” followed by “Investigate promising paths.” A revisable evidence graph grows beside it.]

A commentary in *Nature Machine Intelligence*, discussing the DeepEvidence system, reports that informed, staged exploration can outperform undirected searching across large, heterogeneous knowledge bases. The system first searches broadly, then follows promising mechanistic and citation links in depth. It records entities, relationships, observations, and provenance in an evolving evidence graph. [claim:01-c1]

That does not prove the same workflow will produce more original dissertations. The article is commentary on another system, and its evidence concerns structured exploration—not the long-term quality of researcher-selected topics. Still, it suggests a practical workflow: ask AI to construct a revisable map rather than deliver a verdict.

[Visual: A table with four columns: candidate question, supporting evidence, contradictory evidence, unresolved dependency.]

For each candidate direction, have the system identify relevant literatures, competing findings, assumptions, and missing evidence. Then inspect the primary sources yourself. Provenance can show where an assertion came from; it cannot establish that an apparent gap is important, scientifically tractable, or truly novel.

MIT professor Sasha Rakhlin assigns that evaluative responsibility to the graduate researcher. In his account, students should learn to formulate problems, audit model outputs, reproduce results, and defend their choices. He also distinguishes obtaining a solution from understanding why it works, what generalizes, and what should be asked next. [claim:01-c2]

[Visual: The AI-generated map stops at a gate labeled “Researcher judgment.” Beyond it are three questions: Novel? Significant? Dependency-safe?]

Practitioner testimony points in the same direction. On the *Latent Space* podcast, AI researcher Alex Zhang says that an expert who understands a problem can sometimes guide a model more efficiently than a brute-force search consuming hundreds of billions or even a trillion tokens. He qualifies the observation: the overall trend is not yet clear. [claim:01-c3]

Expertise, on this view, is not merely another input to automate. It determines which branch deserves attention and which attractive-looking premise should be abandoned.

The cost of skipping that judgment can propagate. Retraction Watch reported that, in a large release of AI-generated mathematics manuscripts, roughly half of the results were released unconfirmed. A later sign error invalidated one argument and constructions used by two dependent papers. This episode does not show that AI-assisted topic discovery is generally unreliable. It shows why scale and speed cannot substitute for validation.

[Visual: One faulty premise card connects to several dependent cards; the links turn red.]

Taken together, these sources support a division of labor: use AI to generate candidates, trace connections, expose dependencies, and keep the search revisable. Reserve gap validation and final topic choice for researchers who can test novelty, significance, and the strength of the underlying evidence. [claim:01-c4]

Structured exploration may still reveal genuinely unexpected connections, and future systems may become better judges of novelty. Current evidence, however, does not tell us whether AI-selected questions yield more original dissertations over time.

So the practical rule is simple: let AI enlarge the map. Do not let it decide which destination deserves years of research.
