# Authorship After Automation Means Auditable Responsibility

<!-- claim:03-c1 -->
When an AI system can produce polished prose, surface references, and contribute technical work, sentence-level production becomes a poor proxy for authorship. Sasha Rakhlin argues in an MIT interview that a polished paper is becoming a weaker signal of individual expertise. He proposes that evaluation instead establish what the researcher contributed and accepts intellectual responsibility for, including when AI performed substantial portions of the work. This is an expert position, not a settled authorship rule. It nevertheless identifies the central problem: a reader, supervisor, or examiner needs to know who can defend the work when fluency no longer reveals where the intellectual labor occurred.

<!-- claim:03-c5 -->
The publication’s inference is that responsible authorship should be understood as an auditable chain: contribution, evidence, verification, disclosure, and correction. The human author need not claim to have manually composed every sentence. The author must, however, be able to reconstruct how the question was chosen, distinguish personal contributions from model-generated ones, trace consequential claims to evidence, verify citations and dependencies, disclose material assistance under the relevant venue’s rules, and correct the record when an error is found. Ownership of the final document is weaker than responsibility for the process that made it publishable.

<!-- claim:03-c2 -->
Auditability must not be confused with correctness. A *Nature Machine Intelligence* commentary warns that recording where information originated does not make the resulting conclusion valid. Sources may conflict, use weak methods, reflect publication bias, or apply only in a different context. The commentary’s system records explicit provenance but still relies on a language model to reconcile contradictory evidence. Thus, a detailed trail can answer “Where did this assertion come from?” while leaving “Should we believe it?” unresolved. Authors must evaluate source quality and inferential fit, not merely preserve links.

<!-- claim:03-c3 -->
A reviewer in a commercial SciSpace webinar describes acceptable assistance as leaving the idea, data, and intellectual ownership with the researcher. This is practitioner guidance delivered in a vendor-sponsored setting, not an independently validated boundary. Its useful implication is that editing assistance should not quietly replace the scholarly contribution. If AI reorganizes a paragraph or suggests a clearer expression, the author can still inspect and own the underlying argument. If it supplies the research premise, selects the evidence, resolves contradictions, and writes the conclusion without meaningful human examination, declaring the output “my research” becomes much harder to defend.

<!-- claim:03-c4 -->
Periodic Labs’ founders offer a more expansive model of process records. In a Latent Space interview, they describe capturing conversations, intuitions, laboratory actions, computations, and code so that models can learn from the process of science rather than only its final outputs. The idea also has an accountability benefit: full lineage could make it easier to reconstruct how a result emerged. But this is commercially interested founder testimony about proprietary process data and a laboratory platform. It should not be treated as independent proof that comprehensive logging improves research integrity or establishes who understood a result.

<!-- claim:03-c5 -->
For a PhD student, an authorship record can be selective rather than total. It might connect each major claim to its evidence, identify model-assisted transformations that materially changed an argument, preserve versions of important formulations or code, and record checks performed after AI intervention. The relevant granularity depends on risk. A stylistic edit may need little more than disclosure consistent with venue policy; a generated proof idea, experimental interpretation, or literature synthesis needs enough documentation for the author to reproduce and challenge it. The objective is not bureaucratic completeness but the ability to explain dependencies and assume responsibility for them.

<!-- claim:03-c5 -->
Retraction Watch’s reporting on withdrawn and revised AI-generated mathematics manuscripts shows why responsibility extends beyond the paper being signed. A single sign error reportedly invalidated one manuscript’s argument and constructions used by two dependent papers; other manuscripts required repairs or clarification. This case does not define authorship policy, but it demonstrates that correction obligations follow dependency chains. An author who discovers a flawed lemma, dataset transformation, or generated citation must ask which tables, claims, drafts, and collaborating projects inherited it. Auditable authorship includes the capacity and willingness to propagate corrections.

<!-- claim:03-c4 -->
The counterargument is substantial. Detailed logs can expose confidential ideas, participant data, proprietary code, or informal conversations. They can impose disproportionate administrative work and create surveillance-like records that reward documentation rather than understanding. Logs can also be gamed: a complete transcript proves that events were recorded, not that the named author grasped their significance. Disciplines differ in acceptable disclosure and evidence standards, so one universal logging threshold could obscure rather than clarify genuine contribution.

<!-- claim:03-c1 -->
The better standard is proportionate, contestable accountability. Supervisors, coauthors, and venues should ask whether the researcher can explain the work, reproduce central results, identify AI’s material contributions, defend evidentiary choices, and repair errors. Process records support those tests but do not replace them. As automation weakens the evidentiary value of polished prose, authorship becomes less about possession of sentences and more about answerability for the knowledge claim placed into circulation.
