# Use AI to Widen the Search, Not to Choose the Question

<!-- claim:01-c1 -->
For a PhD student, topic selection is not merely an information-retrieval problem. It requires deciding which unanswered question is consequential, tractable, and sufficiently original to justify years of work. AI can nevertheless improve the search preceding that decision. A *Nature Machine Intelligence* commentary reports that staged exploration, supported by an evolving evidence graph, beat undirected searching across large, heterogeneous knowledge bases. That is a reported result about the system discussed in the commentary—not proof that the same method will produce better dissertation topics. Its practical lesson is narrower: literature exploration benefits from preserving entities, relationships, observations, and provenance in a revisable external structure instead of asking a model for a single authoritative list of gaps.

<!-- claim:01-c4 -->
A useful division of labor follows. Let an AI system broaden the candidate set, cluster neighboring literatures, expose assumptions, and record which sources support each proposed direction. Then require the researcher to validate whether an apparent gap exists, whether adjacent fields have already addressed it under different terminology, and whether resolving it would change theory or practice. This is the publication’s inference from the sources, not a directly tested prescription. The distinction matters because provenance can show where a claim came from without establishing that the claim is correct, representative, or important.

<!-- claim:01-c2 -->
Sasha Rakhlin’s MIT interview places the irreducibly scholarly work on the student’s side of that boundary. He argues that graduate researchers should learn to formulate problems, audit model outputs, reproduce results, and defend their choices. This is an expert perspective rather than a controlled comparison of AI-assisted and unaided students. Still, it captures why accepting a generated research question is hazardous: the student may inherit premises, definitions, and evaluation criteria without developing the judgment needed to challenge them. A candidate topic becomes the researcher’s question only when the researcher can explain its dependencies, plausible failure modes, and value beyond benchmark improvement.

<!-- claim:01-c3 -->
Practitioner testimony points in the same direction from an efficiency angle. In a Latent Space interview, AI researcher Alex Zhang suggests that someone who understands a problem can sometimes guide a model toward an insight that would otherwise require an enormous brute-force token search. He explicitly presents the trend as uncertain, so this should not be read as measured evidence that expert steering always wins. It instead identifies a productive role for domain knowledge: expertise supplies constraints and discriminating questions, while the model supplies breadth. Compute may generate thousands of directions; informed judgment can reject families of directions that rest on the wrong abstraction.

<!-- claim:01-c4 -->
For AI and operations-research students, this suggests treating topic discovery as a sequence of falsifiable filters. An AI-generated map might connect a new decomposition method to scheduling, routing, or mechanism-design literatures. The student’s task is then to inspect the cited work, reconstruct the closest baselines, identify what assumptions create the alleged opening, and state what evidence would show that the opening is illusory. The map should remain revisable: a discovered paper may collapse one branch, while a contradictory result may create a better question elsewhere. AI has improved the process if it makes those revisions cheaper and more legible, not merely if it produces more topic statements.

<!-- claim:01-c4 -->
The cost of confusing generation with validation appears in Retraction Watch’s reporting on a large release of AI-generated mathematics manuscripts. According to that report, roughly half of the results were released unconfirmed, and subsequent errors affected multiple dependent manuscripts. This episode does not establish that AI-selected research topics are generally poor. It does illustrate the structural danger of scaling outputs faster than dependencies can be checked: one weak premise can propagate through an apparently diverse portfolio. In topic selection, the analogous failure is to generate many projects whose novelty claims all depend on the same incomplete search or misunderstood result.

<!-- claim:01-c1 -->
There is a real counterargument. Structured exploration may uncover connections that an expert, constrained by established disciplinary categories, would never consider. Future systems may also become better novelty judges. The present evidence does not measure whether AI-assisted selection yields more original dissertations over time, however, and the Nature article is commentary on another system rather than an independent longitudinal evaluation. Nor does a clean evidence graph establish scientific significance. A well-documented gap can still be trivial.

<!-- claim:01-c2 -->
The responsible use, then, is expansive but provisional. Use AI to make the search space larger, organized, and easier to interrogate. Keep a record of candidate questions and the evidence that strengthened or killed them. But reserve the decisive claims—“this is new,” “this matters,” and “this is worth pursuing”—for a researcher prepared to reproduce the search, consult the underlying literature, and defend the choice. The efficiency gain comes from accelerating exploration; originality and responsibility remain attached to the act of judgment.
