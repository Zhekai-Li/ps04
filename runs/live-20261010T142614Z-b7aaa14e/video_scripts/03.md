# Preserve Responsibility by Recording the Research Process

**Estimated narration: 4 minutes**

[Visual: A finished paper zooms outward to reveal a process record: searches, source decisions, failed attempts, computations, corrections, and author approvals.]

A disclosure can say AI was used. It cannot show what the AI did, which evidence was checked, or who made consequential judgments.

Responsible assistance therefore requires two things: a bounded division of labor and a durable record of the research process.

MIT’s expert perspective says evaluation should identify what the researcher contributed and accepts intellectual responsibility for, including when AI performed substantial parts of the work. [claim:03-c1]

[Visual: Two columns labeled “Delegated operation” and “Author contribution.” Place an explicit approval checkpoint between them.]

Ask sharper questions: Which tasks were delegated? Which outputs entered the paper? Who verified and interpreted them? Who can defend every material choice?

A SciSpace practitioner testimonial proposes outsourcing tedious literature work while retaining human thinking, analysis, reasoning, judgment, and deeper shaping of the paper. [claim:03-c4] This is a useful description of task boundaries, but it comes from commercially published testimony and should not be treated as independent evidence of product performance.

Delegate reversible, inspectable operations such as generating search terms, clustering abstracts, formatting notes, or proposing outlines. Keep problem formulation, evidence acceptance, analysis, interpretation, and final claims under author control.

[Visual: Reversible tasks move into an “AI-assisted” lane. Scientific judgments remain in a “researcher accountable” lane.]

A Periodic Labs founder describes full process lineage—including conversations, intuitions, laboratory actions, computations, and code—as valuable for learning from scientific work rather than only its final product. [claim:03-c2]

The same founders describe sequences of negative experimental results as useful process evidence for understanding how iteration eventually produced a positive result. [claim:03-c3]

This is commercially interested protocol testimony, not independent performance validation. Still, failed searches, rejected interpretations, and corrected calculations can explain the final approach and prevent discarded errors from returning.

[Visual: A chronological research log with entries marked “tested,” “rejected,” “revised,” and “accepted.” Do not display invented experimental results.]

Retraction Watch reported that, in an AI-associated mathematics release, OpenAI found errors during an audit, revised manuscripts, and withdrew papers when errors could not be fixed. [claim:03-c5]

The case does not show how often records prevent errors. It shows why researchers need enough lineage to locate dependencies and issue corrections.

The combined evidence supports a process record, not disclosure alone. Log delegated tasks, prompts or specifications, source lineage, failed approaches, verification, corrections, and final judgments. This is a cross-source inference, not a universal policy. [claim:03-c6]

[Visual: A compact record template appears with seven fields: date; task; tool or model; inputs and data restrictions; output used; verification; author decision.]

A SciSpace reviewer also warns researchers to consider privacy, ethics, institutional review board requirements, and intellectual property before putting data into an AI system.

Disclose material AI assistance under applicable journal, university, funder, and disciplinary rules. There is no universal format: the process log is the internal trail, while disclosure is the required public account.

Efficiency is compatible with responsibility only when the author can reconstruct the path from delegated task to published claim—and defend the judgments made along that path.
