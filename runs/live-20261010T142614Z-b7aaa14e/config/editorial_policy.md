# Scholar in the Loop editorial policy — version 3

## Purpose and audience

Select recent evidence that helps AI and operations-research PhD students decide where AI assistance genuinely improves topic discovery or academic writing, and where it shifts rather than removes intellectual work. Assume readers already use LLMs and need discriminating guidance rather than introductory tool lists.

## Selection rules

1. **Relevance.** Accept only items that bear directly on topic discovery, research-gap evaluation, outlining, drafting, revision, citation verification, disclosure, originality, evidentiary rigor, or author responsibility.
2. **Freshness.** Mechanical date eligibility is necessary but not sufficient. A recent page that merely recycles an old event is rejected or deferred unless it reports a new result, policy, implementation detail, or credible retrospective evidence.
3. **Claim-specific credibility.** Judge the method and evidence behind each claim. Prestige, popularity, production quality, and model confidence are not substitutes for support. Separate measured results from product demonstrations, forecasts, and personal experience.
4. **Novelty.** Prefer items that add a new finding, constraint, counterexample, or implementation lesson relative to stored claims. Repetition of the same announcement is not corroboration.
5. **Independence.** Trace repeated reports to the underlying study or announcement. Treat podcast/video copies and syndication as one underlying item. Use publisher and `underlying_id` metadata, plus explicit identity overrides, to avoid inflated counts.
6. **Perspective diversity.** Across the series, include research, policy, commercial, and practitioner views when evidence exists. Do not treat a vendor claim as independent validation of its own product.
7. **Incentives and conflicts.** Name relevant commercial, institutional, or advocacy incentives. Lower confidence when claims are unsupported, methods are unavailable, or evaluation data are self-selected.
8. **Uncertainty and disagreement.** State what is measured, what is claimed, what is inferred, and what remains unknown. Explain plausible reasons for conflict; do not manufacture balance.
9. **Original synthesis.** Accept material only when it can support or challenge a cross-source argument. A list of summaries is not an editorial contribution.
10. **Exclusions.** Exclude undergraduate cheating, generic workplace productivity, automated paper generation, fabricated citations, and unsupervised acceptance of AI conclusions.
11. **Perspective evidence.** Accept directly relevant personal experience or stakeholder testimony when it contains a specific qualitative perspective that can contribute to cross-source synthesis, provided the source and relevant incentives are disclosed. An unsupported quantitative or promotional claim in the same item is not, by itself, grounds to discard an otherwise usable qualitative perspective. Explicitly exclude the unsupported claim from evidentiary use, label the accepted material as testimony or perspective, and assign it only to pieces where that perspective is useful. Without independent corroboration, do not use testimony to support quantitative performance, prevalence, or causal claims. Briefs and final pieces must preserve these limits rather than converting testimony into a measured result. Reject testimony that is irrelevant, too vague for synthesis, or offers no usable content beyond its unsupported claim.

## Decision meanings

- `accept`: sufficiently relevant and supportable for one or more specified pieces, including clearly bounded perspective evidence under rule 11.
- `reject`: irrelevant, duplicative without new evidence, promotional without usable support, or within an explicit exclusion.
- `defer`: potentially relevant but inaccessible, ambiguous, inadequately supported, or better evaluated after corroboration.

Every decision must name the decisive evidence or limitation. Every accepted item must be assigned to at least one piece; rejected and deferred items must have no piece IDs.
