# Use AI to Expand the Search Space, Not Choose the Research Bet

<!-- claim:01-c1 -->
For topic discovery, the strongest use of AI is not asking, “What should my dissertation be about?” It is asking the system to construct a navigable map of what might be worth investigating. A *Nature Machine Intelligence* commentary describes a staged design: breadth-first exploration surveys multiple knowledge resources for relevant entities, while depth-first exploration follows promising mechanistic and citation links. This is a source’s account of an evidence-exploration system, not proof that the method will identify important research questions in every field. Still, the distinction is useful for AI and operations-research students. A model can cheaply expand the candidate set, group related work, surface conflicting findings, and identify dependencies that deserve closer reading.

<!-- claim:01-c5 -->
The publication’s inference is that this map should remain an intermediate artifact. A practical session might begin with a deliberately broad prompt covering adjacent methods, applications, assumptions, and negative findings. The student could then request an evidence graph linking candidate questions to original papers, datasets, mathematical assumptions, and unresolved contradictions. Promising branches would receive deeper searches; weak branches would be retained as rejected alternatives with reasons. None of this establishes that a candidate is significant or original. It converts an initially vague literature search into an auditable collection of research possibilities.

<!-- claim:01-c2 -->
Problem selection remains a human scholarly responsibility because it requires judgments that are not reducible to retrieval. An MIT expert perspective says students should learn to formulate problems, audit model outputs, reproduce results, and defend their choices. That is a normative institutional view rather than outcome data, but it identifies the intellectual work at risk when topic selection is delegated wholesale. A defensible proposal should therefore explain why the problem matters, what existing approaches fail to capture, which assumptions make it tractable, and what result would change the field’s understanding. “The model found a gap” is not such an explanation.

<!-- claim:01-c3 -->
There is also a strategic reason not to let a model rank topics for you. In a Latent Space interview, Alex Zhang argues from his PhD experience that academia’s distinctive opportunity lies in unconventional, high-risk questions rather than subjects already aligned with current industry priorities. This is informed practitioner testimony, not measured evidence that risky bets yield better dissertations. Yet it exposes a weakness in model-mediated selection: a system trained on the visible literature may preferentially generate legible, densely represented extensions. Those can be feasible and publishable while still missing the unusual bet that gives academic research its comparative value.

<!-- claim:01-c4 -->
Apparent productivity also cannot substitute for verification. Retraction Watch reported that OpenAI withdrew three mathematics manuscripts after a sign error invalidated one argument and affected constructions used by two dependent papers; the company also revised 14 other manuscripts. This is a reported incident, not a complete audit of the release. Its lesson for topic discovery is nevertheless concrete: a promising chain of deductions can inherit a local error. If an AI-generated direction relies on theorem A, benchmark B, or an empirical regularity in paper C, the student must inspect and, where material, reproduce that dependency before treating the direction as feasible.

<!-- claim:01-c5 -->
The resulting division of labor is demanding but clear. Let AI enumerate neighboring literatures, propose alternative formulations, expose citation chains, and maintain a record of why branches were opened or closed. Require the researcher to choose the bet and defend its significance, originality, feasibility, and verification plan. For an operations-research project, that may mean independently checking whether the claimed gap survives changes in objective, information structure, or computational assumptions. For an AI project, it may mean confirming that a supposed capability gap is not an artifact of one benchmark or evaluation protocol.

<!-- claim:01-c1 -->
A limitation is that even a provenance-bearing evidence graph can create false reassurance. The DeepEvidence discussion is a scholarly commentary rather than an independent prospective replication, and organized provenance does not prove that a source is correct or appropriately interpreted. Zhang’s argument favors ambition but does not tell students how much risk a dissertation can absorb. MIT’s perspective likewise anticipates future practices without establishing their effects. AI-assisted exploration should therefore be judged by whether it improves the researcher’s questions and audit trail—not by the number of candidate topics it produces.
