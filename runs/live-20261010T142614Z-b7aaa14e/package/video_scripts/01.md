# Use AI to Expand the Search Space, Not Choose the Research Bet

**Estimated narration: 4 minutes**

[Visual: A blank research map expands into clusters of papers, questions, mechanisms, and citation links. Label it “candidate space,” not “answers.”]

A language model can propose fifty dissertation topics before you finish describing one. That speed is useful—but it can also disguise the hardest part of research: deciding which problem deserves years of attention.

The defensible role for AI is to expand and organize the search space. The research bet remains yours.

A Nature Machine Intelligence commentary describes a staged exploration design. It begins breadth-first, surveying multiple knowledge resources for potentially relevant entities. It then switches to depth-first exploration, following mechanistic and citation links around promising directions. [claim:01-c1]

[Visual: Simple two-stage diagram: “Broad survey” → “Deep evidence chains.” Animate paper titles and links without displaying fabricated results.]

For a PhD student, that suggests a practical workflow. Ask AI to map neighboring literatures, propose competing framings, identify assumptions, and trace citation chains. Then inspect the original sources. Keep an evidence table showing which direction came from where and which claims remain uncertain.

But do not confuse a map with a destination—or provenance with proof. The DeepEvidence discussion is a scholarly commentary, not an independent prospective replication. Even a graph that records where information came from does not establish that its conclusions are correct.

MIT’s graduate-training perspective draws the human boundary clearly: students should formulate problems, audit model outputs, reproduce results, and defend their choices. [claim:01-c2]

[Visual: Four cards appear: “Formulate,” “Audit,” “Reproduce,” “Defend.” A researcher—not an AI icon—signs off on each card.]

This boundary matters for originality. On the Latent Space podcast, MIT researcher Alex Zhang argues from his PhD experience that academia’s distinctive opportunity is to pursue unconventional, high-risk questions rather than topics already optimized for current industry priorities. He presents this as informed practitioner judgment, not measured evidence about dissertation outcomes. [claim:01-c3]

AI systems tend to make accessible patterns easier to see. That can broaden discovery, but it does not decide whether a neglected question is profound, trivial, infeasible, or merely unfashionable. Those judgments require disciplinary knowledge, taste, and responsibility.

Verification is equally important. Retraction Watch reported that OpenAI withdrew three mathematics manuscripts after a sign error invalidated an argument in one paper and affected the construction used by two dependent papers. The report also said fourteen other manuscripts were revised. [claim:01-c4]

[Visual: A dependency diagram with three generic manuscript boxes. One assumption turns red; the dependent links illuminate. Do not show invented equations or error rates.]

That case does not measure the reliability of AI-assisted mathematics as a whole. It does show how a local error can propagate through apparently productive work—and why exploration volume cannot replace dependency checking.

Taken together, these sources support a disciplined division of labor: use AI to generate and structure candidate directions, while reserving topic selection for the researcher who can justify significance, originality, dependencies, feasibility, and a verification plan. This is a synthesis of the sources, not a direct experimental result. [claim:01-c5]

Before committing to a topic, answer five questions in your own words: What is the scientific problem? Why does it matter? What makes the proposed contribution non-obvious? Which evidence and assumptions does it depend on? And what would falsify or substantially weaken the direction?

[Visual: The five questions form a decision gate. Many candidate topics enter; one provisional research bet exits, stamped “researcher justified.”]

AI can make the landscape larger and easier to navigate. It should not make the intellectual commitment for you.
