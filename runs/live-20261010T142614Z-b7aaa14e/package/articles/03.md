# Preserve Responsibility by Recording the Research Process

<!-- claim:03-c1 -->
Authorship cannot be inferred from a manuscript’s polish. An MIT expert perspective argues that evaluation should identify what a researcher contributed and accepts responsibility for, including when AI performed substantial work. This institutional position is not evidence that one documentation system improves outcomes. It nevertheless identifies the central test: the author must be able to reconstruct and defend consequential decisions. Declaring that an LLM was used does not show where it influenced the argument or how its output was checked.

<!-- claim:03-c4 -->
A practitioner testimonial published by SciSpace proposes outsourcing tedious literature work while keeping human thinking, analysis, reasoning, judgment, and deeper shaping of the paper. This commercial testimony does not independently validate the product or its claimed acceleration, but the division itself is useful. AI can normalize references, cluster results, compare terminology, suggest outlines, or flag passages. The researcher should retain decisions about problem formulation, inclusion criteria, assumptions, interpretation, evidentiary weight, and final claims.

<!-- claim:03-c2 -->
Responsibility becomes inspectable when the process leaves a record. A Periodic Labs founder describes full scientific lineage—including conversations, intuitions, laboratory actions, computations, and code—as valuable for learning from science rather than only final outputs. This comes from a commercially interested participant discussing a proprietary platform, not independent evaluation. Applied to writing, it suggests recording delegated tasks, model and prompt context, retrieved sources, transformations, and the researcher’s acceptance or rejection decisions.

<!-- claim:03-c3 -->
Failed approaches belong in that lineage. Periodic Labs’ founders describe sequences of negative experimental results as useful evidence for understanding the iterations that eventually produce a positive result. This is source testimony, not proof that recording every failed attempt is always worth its cost. Yet selective retention distorts both learning and accountability. If an AI-generated formulation failed because a constraint was omitted, or a literature lead collapsed when the original paper was opened, preserving that episode can prevent repetition and explain why the final design differs from seemingly obvious alternatives.

<!-- claim:03-c5 -->
Correction history matters for the same reason. Retraction Watch reported that OpenAI identified errors in a mathematics release during an audit, revised manuscripts, and withdrew three papers when errors were found. The article documents a particular release rather than the general effectiveness of process records. Still, correction requires traceability: an author must be able to find which claims inherit a faulty sign, citation, dataset version, or model-generated summary. Without a dependency record, discovering an error does not reveal the full scope of what needs repair.

<!-- claim:03-c6 -->
The publication’s inference is that accountable AI use needs a durable process record, not disclosure alone. A compact record can identify the delegated task, available sources or data, returned output, verification method, rejected alternatives, uncertainty, affected sections, and final human judgment. It should also log corrections and dependencies. The purpose is to let authors, collaborators, advisers, or auditors reconstruct how a material claim entered the work and why it survived review.

<!-- claim:03-c6 -->
Such a record should be paired with policy-sensitive disclosure. The manuscript can summarize material assistance at the level required by the relevant journal, institution, funder, or discipline, while the fuller internal record supports later questions. Researchers must also avoid placing confidential data, unpublished collaborator material, personal information, or protected intellectual property into systems without appropriate authorization. The available sources do not establish one universal disclosure format, so compliance cannot be reduced to a standard paragraph copied between papers.

<!-- claim:03-c1 -->
There is a real counterargument: exhaustive logging can burden researchers, expose sensitive intellectual development, and produce archives too large to inspect. Not every spelling correction or formatting suggestion warrants preservation. A proportional rule is more defensible: document assistance that affects the research question, evidence selection, analysis, technical claims, interpretation, or attribution, plus anything difficult to reproduce later. Routine low-consequence edits can be summarized. The dividing line should be whether an omitted interaction would make it materially harder to explain or audit the paper.

<!-- claim:03-c6 -->
For PhD training, the practical implication is deeper than compliance. A process record makes intellectual ownership visible. It shows whether the student merely accepted a generated synthesis or noticed a contradiction, redesigned the formulation, rejected an attractive but unsupported claim, and defended the resulting choice. That evidence can support supervision and evaluation while preserving AI’s efficiency benefits. Responsibility survives automation when the researcher can show—not merely assert—what the machine did, what the evidence established, what failed, what changed, and which judgments remained their own.
