# Authorship after AI: responsibility cannot be delegated

AI complicates the evidence of authorship without changing its central obligation. Researchers may delegate searches, candidate proofs, code drafts, summaries, and editorial revisions. They cannot delegate responsibility for what the paper asks readers to believe. The relevant standard is therefore not how many sentences the researcher typed, but whether they can trace, reproduce, explain, and defend the work—and correct or withdraw it when support fails. This is the publication’s inference from the combined evidence. <!-- claim:03-c5 -->

Fluent reasoning text is a particularly weak substitute for that responsibility. The Nature perspective “Characterizing the role of reasoning tokens in large language models as state over tokens” argues that a model’s displayed reasoning can be a plausible derivation of an answer without faithfully describing the process that produced it. This is the authors’ conceptual and evidence-based claim, not proof that every displayed derivation is false or useless. Such text may still help a researcher inspect individual steps, but it should not be treated as a model audit log. <!-- claim:03-c1 -->

That distinction matters whenever an author says the model “reasoned” its way to a result. A derivation can be mathematically checkable even if it is not a faithful causal account of the system. Conversely, a convincing narrative can contain a hidden gap. The author must therefore validate the derivation on its own terms—by proof checking, execution, replication, or comparison with sources—rather than relying on the apparent transparency of the generated rationale. The broader inference is that plausibility should trigger inspection, not calibrated trust by itself. <!-- claim:03-c1 --><!-- claim:03-c5 -->

MIT’s Alexander Rakhlin frames academic evaluation around identifiable contribution and intellectual responsibility, including when substantial parts of the work were performed by AI. That is a source claim about how universities should assess researchers. It shifts disclosure away from a crude word-count model. A student might type little yet make the decisive contribution by formulating the problem, rejecting invalid approaches, designing the evaluation, and defending the conclusion. Another might edit every generated sentence while remaining unable to explain the assumptions or reproduce the result. <!-- claim:03-c2 -->

The practical test is control over evidence and decisions. An author should know which claims came from which sources, which computations were executed, which model suggestions were rejected, and what changed after a failed experiment. Where applicable policies require disclosure, material AI assistance should be reported accurately. The supplied evidence does not establish a universal disclosure threshold, so researchers must consult the relevant journal, conference, institution, and funder rather than infer a single rule from general principles. <!-- claim:03-c2 --><!-- claim:03-c5 -->

The consequences of weak control are illustrated by Retraction Watch’s report on OpenAI’s release of mathematics manuscripts. An OpenAI spokesperson said roughly half of the results were released unconfirmed; the report followed withdrawals and revisions prompted by errors. This is a reported statement about one unusually large release, not evidence that half of AI-assisted research generally fails. It nevertheless shows why scale changes the burden of responsibility: when hundreds of outputs and their dependencies appear at once, rapid correction does not replace verification before release. <!-- claim:03-c3 -->

A reviewer speaking in a SciSpace video offers a complementary standard: AI should help produce good research, not merely more research. This is practitioner guidance, and the publisher has a commercial interest in AI-mediated research workflows. Its value is normative rather than statistical. For a PhD student, increased volume is worthwhile only if it expands tested alternatives, improves clarity, or strengthens evidence. More drafts, citations, and candidate theorems are liabilities when the author cannot inspect them at the rate they are produced. <!-- claim:03-c4 -->

Process lineage offers one way to make responsibility visible. Periodic Labs says it records conversations, intuitions, laboratory actions, computations, and code so that models learn from the process rather than only the final product. That is a company account, not independent proof of effectiveness. Adapted cautiously, the idea supports an authorship record containing claim-to-source links, experiment versions, decision notes, and the reasons for accepting or rejecting model output. Such a record need not be public in full to help an author answer a reviewer or investigate an error. <!-- claim:03-c5 -->

There are limits. Human authorship has never guaranteed correctness, and requiring exhaustive logs can expose confidential data, peer-review material, security-sensitive prompts, or proprietary methods. A displayed derivation can still be useful even if it is not a faithful account of model internals. The right response is proportionate auditability: preserve enough lineage to substantiate important claims and contributions while respecting privacy and legal constraints. Responsibility is not equivalent to indiscriminate surveillance of the research process. <!-- claim:03-c1 --><!-- claim:03-c5 -->

Authorship after AI is thus less about guarding the keyboard than owning the epistemic chain. Researchers should be able to say what the system contributed, what they themselves decided, how each important claim was tested, and what evidence would change their conclusion. If they cannot, disclosure alone does not cure the problem. If they can, substantial AI assistance need not erase authorship—but it does make the author’s burden of verification and explanation more explicit. <!-- claim:03-c2 --><!-- claim:03-c5 -->
