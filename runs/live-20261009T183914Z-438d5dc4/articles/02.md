# Replace one-shot drafting with a verified research pipeline

The defensible unit of AI assistance is a pipeline, not a passage. A generated paragraph may be elegant while concealing a broken citation, an untested computation, or a dependency on an earlier error. A research pipeline instead exposes inputs, transformations, tools, tests, and revisions. Across the supplied sources, the publication’s inference is that efficiency and rigor are compatible when verification occurs during generation rather than after the prose has acquired an undeserved appearance of completion. <!-- claim:02-c5 -->

LACE provides quantitative evidence for this principle in algorithm design. Its modular framework defined input and output schemas, a tool library, a verified problem contract, and an iterative process for generating and selecting heuristics. The authors reported a Valid Rate of 0.944; none of the compared LLM-based baselines exceeded 0.611. This is a reported benchmark result, not evidence about prose drafting directly. It nevertheless shows that decomposing work and checking feasibility can produce far more reliable outputs than asking the same class of model to solve the whole task in one pass. <!-- claim:02-c1 -->

For writing, the analogous pipeline begins with claims rather than sentences. A draft section should be treated as a collection of propositions linked to primary sources, computations, or clearly labeled interpretation. The model can propose an outline, identify missing transitions, or produce alternative explanations. Before those suggestions enter the manuscript, externally checkable claims should be compared with the underlying papers, and numerical results should be reproduced from code or data. Polishing comes after the claim-to-evidence structure survives inspection. This workflow is an inference from evidence across several domains, not a validated recipe with a measured effect size. <!-- claim:02-c5 -->

The danger of postponing verification appears in a Retraction Watch report about OpenAI’s mass release of mathematics manuscripts. According to the report, OpenAI withdrew three manuscripts after a sign error invalidated an argument in one and constructions used by two dependent papers. The company also reported revisions to 14 other manuscripts. These are reported facts about a particular release, not an estimated base rate for AI-generated mathematics. Their importance lies in the dependency structure: one local error can contaminate several downstream results when generation proceeds faster than validation. <!-- claim:02-c2 -->

Citation checking is a simpler instance of the same problem. In a SciSpace video, a journal and grant reviewer advises researchers to test every AI-supplied DOI and confirm that it resolves to the intended work. This is practitioner guidance rather than controlled evidence, and SciSpace sells research software. Even so, the test is concrete and falsifiable. A DOI that resolves is not sufficient—the destination must have the claimed authors, result, and relevance—but failure at this first check immediately exposes a defective citation. <!-- claim:02-c3 -->

Provenance should cover more than the bibliography. In a Latent Space interview, representatives of Periodic Labs say their system records conversations, intuitions, laboratory actions, computations, and code so models can learn from the process of science rather than only final outputs. This is the company’s description of its practice, not independent validation of its platform. The useful principle is lineage: when a conclusion changes, a researcher should be able to locate the affected evidence, analysis, and prose instead of rereading a polished manuscript as though it were the complete history of the work. <!-- claim:02-c4 -->

For an AI or operations-research project, this might mean maintaining a claim ledger beside the manuscript. Each consequential statement points to a source location, an experiment identifier, or an executable test; model-generated alternatives remain labeled until accepted; and dependent claims are rechecked after any correction. This is not clerical maximalism. It lets researchers revise locally, identify which conclusions survive a failed assumption, and use models for rapid iteration without confusing throughput with confidence. <!-- claim:02-c4 --><!-- claim:02-c5 -->

The counterargument is cost. Full formal verification is unavailable for much empirical work, and checking every minor sentence can consume the time AI was meant to save. Relevance, novelty, and explanatory value also resist mechanical tests. LACE’s success on combinatorial optimization does not show that every scholarly judgment can be encoded as a contract. A sensible pipeline therefore allocates scrutiny according to consequence: central claims, citations, computations, and dependencies receive explicit checks, while stylistic suggestions receive lighter review. <!-- claim:02-c1 --><!-- claim:02-c5 -->

This leaves the researcher with more responsibility, not less. AI can accelerate candidate generation, repair, and revision, but a polished output is only an interface. Confidence should come from the visible chain beneath it: evidence inspected, computations rerun, references resolved, failures retained, and revisions propagated. The efficiency gain is not avoiding scrutiny; it is arranging the work so that scrutiny happens before errors become structural. <!-- claim:02-c2 --><!-- claim:02-c5 -->
