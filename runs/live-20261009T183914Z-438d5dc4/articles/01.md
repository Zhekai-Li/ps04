# Use AI to widen the search, not choose the research bet

For topic selection, the most defensible role for AI is to enlarge and organize the possibility space. A model can propose alternative formulations, connect a problem to adjacent literatures, identify assumptions, and generate tests that might quickly eliminate weak directions. It should not decide which question deserves several years of a researcher’s attention. That decision combines novelty, feasibility, scholarly value, and personal conviction—judgments that a fluent ranking of candidate topics cannot settle. This is the publication’s inference from the evidence, not a demonstrated universal rule. <!-- claim:01-c5 -->

The strongest quantitative evidence comes from a narrower setting. On 36 combinatorial-optimization problems, the LACE framework reported an average score of 0.945, versus 0.870 for the strongest LLM-based comparator and 0.571 for direct prompting. These are reported results from the framework’s authors, who evaluated methods using a shared model backbone. LACE did not simply ask a model for a finished algorithm: it specified a verified problem contract, supplied tools, decomposed the task, and iteratively selected complementary heuristics. The comparison suggests that structure and testing can matter more than one-shot model output. <!-- claim:01-c1 -->

That result offers a useful analogy for topic discovery. Instead of asking, “What should my dissertation be about?”, a student could define constraints such as available data, acceptable computational cost, relevant theory, and the kind of contribution sought. The model could then generate competing problem statements, search for nearby work, and propose falsification tests. A Nature commentary describes a related approach to evidence exploration: staged search, an evolving evidence graph, and a revisable checklist updated as new findings arrive. This is the commentary authors’ proposed model of investigation, not proof that such a workflow identifies important research questions. <!-- claim:01-c3 -->

The human role begins well before final approval. An MIT expert argues that routine calculations, coding, failed approaches, and small discoveries help graduate students acquire intuition; delegating them can remove formative experiences even while enabling more ambitious projects. On this view, problem formulation and auditing are part of the education, not merely overhead to automate away. For an AI or operations-research student, repeatedly discovering why an apparently attractive formulation is ill-posed may build exactly the judgment needed to recognize a worthwhile topic later. This is a source claim about graduate training, rather than a controlled estimate of learning loss. <!-- claim:01-c2 -->

Topic choice also reflects institutional incentives. In a Latent Space interview, Alex Zhang attributes some of academia’s distinctiveness to pursuing problems that industry labs are not currently optimizing for, while warning that graduate students may gravitate toward work that looks attractive to those labs. That is practitioner testimony, not controlled evidence, and the technology-focused publisher benefits from interest in agent systems. Still, it identifies a real failure mode: an AI search tool trained on visible, popular work may make crowded benchmarks look more promising precisely because they are easier to retrieve and summarize. <!-- claim:01-c4 -->

A workable division of labor follows. Let the model generate several research bets, including unfashionable or adversarial alternatives, but require an evidence record for each: closest prior work, unresolved contradiction, required resources, decisive experiment, and conditions under which the idea is no longer novel. The student should inspect the cited papers, test computational assumptions, and write the final rationale in terms they can defend. AI can compress the search, but the researcher must decide which uncertainties are scientifically productive rather than merely inconvenient. <!-- claim:01-c3 --><!-- claim:01-c5 -->

There is an important counterargument. Human control does not guarantee originality: advisers and students can be conservative, status-driven, or unaware of adjacent fields. Nor does LACE establish that constrained AI search improves dissertation selection; its benchmark concerns algorithm construction, uses one shared model backbone, and includes new problems designed by its authors. Evidence graphs improve traceability without proving that retrieved evidence is correct or that a topic matters. The lesson is therefore conditional: AI-assisted exploration is valuable when it makes alternatives and assumptions inspectable, not when its ranked answer substitutes for judgment. <!-- claim:01-c1 --><!-- claim:01-c3 --><!-- claim:01-c5 -->

The cost of ignoring that distinction is that generation scales faster than verification. Retraction Watch reported a case in which a sign error invalidated one mathematical argument and constructions used by two dependent manuscripts. That episode does not estimate the general error rate of AI-assisted research, but it shows how dependencies can propagate a mistake across polished outputs. Topic discovery should exploit scale while ideas are still cheap to discard; commitment should wait for human scrutiny of novelty, feasibility, and evidence. <!-- claim:01-c5 -->
