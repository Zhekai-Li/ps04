# Use AI to widen the search, not choose the research bet

[Visual: A narrow search box expands into a branching map of candidate research questions.]

A language model can produce twenty dissertation ideas before you finish your coffee. That is useful—but it is not originality. The valuable question is whether AI can enlarge the space you examine without deciding which intellectual risk you should take.

The strongest case for AI-assisted discovery comes from structured search, not one-shot prompting. In a Nature Machine Intelligence study of combinatorial optimization, the LACE framework divided algorithm design into a verified problem contract, tool library, and portfolio of candidate heuristics. Across 36 benchmark problems, its authors reported an average score of 0.945. The strongest compared LLM-based method scored 0.870, while direct prompting scored 0.571. [claim:01-c1]

[Visual: Three labeled bars—LACE 0.945, strongest LLM baseline 0.870, direct prompting 0.571. Caption: “Reported on 36 CO-Bench problems.”]

This does not show that LACE can choose a dissertation topic. The benchmarks were bounded optimization problems, all LLM-based methods shared one model backbone, and the framework’s authors conducted its evaluation. But the comparison illustrates a transferable principle: models become more useful when a researcher defines constraints, decomposes the search, tests candidates, and iterates.

A Nature commentary describes a related approach to evidence exploration. It highlights staged searching, an evolving evidence graph, and a checklist revised as new findings arrive, instead of commitment after one retrieval pass. [claim:01-c3]

[Visual: Loop diagram: Plan → Search → Record evidence → Replan. Beside it, citations accumulate in a provenance graph.]

For topic discovery, that suggests a practical workflow. Ask AI to generate candidates across different assumptions, methods, datasets, and application areas. Require it to state what evidence would establish novelty, what could falsify the premise, and which dependencies require checking. Then trace every promising claim to primary literature. An evidence graph can improve traceability; it cannot establish that a question is correct, feasible, or important.

The human role is not merely supervisory. MIT professor Alexander Rakhlin argues that routine calculations, coding, failed approaches, and small discoveries help graduate students develop intuition and judgment. In his account, delegating such work can remove formative experiences even while AI enables more ambitious projects. [claim:01-c2]

[Visual: Split screen. Left: “avoidable friction.” Right: “practice that builds judgment.” A researcher decides where each task belongs.]

Problem choice also reflects values and incentives. In a Latent Space interview, MIT researcher Alex Zhang associates distinctive academic work with questions that industry may not be pursuing, while warning that graduate students can gravitate toward topics attractive to industry labs. That is practitioner testimony, not controlled evidence, and the technology-focused publisher benefits from interest in agent systems. Still, it identifies a real question to ask: Is this topic important because of the underlying science—or because it fits today’s benchmark economy? [claim:01-c4]

The danger of scale is visible when generation outruns checking. Retraction Watch reported that a sign error in one AI-produced mathematics manuscript invalidated its argument and constructions used by two dependent papers. This case does not estimate the general error rate of AI-assisted research, but it shows how one unchecked dependency can propagate. [claim:01-c5]

[Visual: One red error node feeds three manuscript cards. Avoid depicting any broader error rate.]

So use AI to widen the search. Have it challenge assumptions, expose neighboring literatures, generate counterarguments, and organize evidence. But reserve three decisions for accountable scholarly judgment: whether the problem matters, whether the contribution is genuinely novel, and whether the evidence supports proceeding.

Human control is no guarantee of originality; people are also conservative and biased. The defensible division of labor is narrower: AI expands and tests possibilities inside explicit constraints. The researcher chooses the bet—and remains responsible for verifying it. [claim:01-c5]
