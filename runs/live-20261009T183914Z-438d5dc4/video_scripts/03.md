# Authorship after AI: responsibility cannot be delegated

[Visual: A manuscript’s “Generate” button changes into four questions: Can you verify it? Reproduce it? Defend it? Correct it?]

AI can search, draft, code, and propose arguments. It cannot assume authorship responsibility. If your name is on the paper, you still need to know what the evidence supports, how the result was produced, and what must happen if the result fails.

That responsibility begins with a distinction between a plausible derivation and a faithful account of model behavior. A Nature Machine Intelligence perspective called “State over Tokens” argues that model-generated reasoning text may explain how an answer could follow through a sequence of steps without explaining the actual process that produced the answer. [claim:03-c1]

[Visual: Two paths reach the same answer. One is labeled “plausible derivation”; the hidden model process remains marked “not established.”]

This does not make every displayed derivation useless. A reader may still inspect individual steps. The warning is about trust calibration: fluent reasoning can increase confidence without justifying it. So do not treat a model’s rationale as a faithful audit log merely because it sounds systematic.

Authorship must therefore be grounded elsewhere—in evidence, reproducibility, and identifiable decisions. MIT professor Alexander Rakhlin argues that academic evaluation should determine what a researcher contributed and takes intellectual responsibility for, including when substantial parts of the work were performed by AI. [claim:03-c2]

[Visual: Contribution record with columns for question formulation, source verification, computation, interpretation, and final approval.]

That standard is more useful than counting keystrokes. A researcher might type little yet make the decisive conceptual and evidentiary contributions. Another might edit every sentence while failing to understand the derivation. Responsible authorship asks whether the named researcher can reproduce the work, defend its assumptions, explain its dependencies, and identify what the AI contributed.

The stakes become clearer at scale. Retraction Watch reported that OpenAI said roughly half the results in a release of more than 700 mathematics manuscripts were unconfirmed. Less than 24 hours after the release, three manuscripts were withdrawn following errors, and other manuscripts were corrected. [claim:03-c3]

[Visual: More than 700 neutral document icons appear; approximately half receive an “unconfirmed” label based specifically on OpenAI’s reported statement. Three are then marked “withdrawn.”]

This episode is not evidence that half of all AI-assisted research is wrong. It is a specific report about one mass release. It does show that polished manuscripts and high output volume do not substitute for confirmation.

A reviewer speaking in a SciSpace webinar makes the normative point plainly: AI should help produce good research, not merely more research. [claim:03-c4] The same reviewer tells PhD researchers that they are becoming the subject experts. That practitioner guidance is not controlled evidence, and SciSpace has a commercial interest in AI-mediated research workflows. But the advice fits the responsibility attached to authorship: expertise must remain visible in the decisions and checks.

[Visual: Counter slows down beside “paper volume”; a checklist fills beside “research quality.”]

What should an author preserve? Not a supposed transcript of the model’s hidden thought process. Preserve the auditable lineage available to you: prompts when materially relevant, retrieved sources, source passages, code and parameters, test outputs, rejected alternatives, changed assumptions, and human approval points. In a Latent Space interview, Periodic Labs describes recording conversations, intuitions, laboratory actions, computations, and code to capture the process of science rather than only its final output. That is the company’s account of its approach, not independent validation. [claim:03-c5]

Detailed logging also has limits. Confidential peer review, participant privacy, cybersecurity, and proprietary data may prohibit retaining or disclosing some material. Disclosure rules vary across journals and institutions, and the supplied evidence does not establish one universal threshold for reporting AI use. Researchers must follow the policies that apply to the specific venue and project.

The combined evidence supports a practical standard: neither fluent rationales nor polished manuscripts demonstrate faithful reasoning, verification, or human contribution. Responsible AI-assisted authorship requires an auditable link from claims to evidence and from major decisions to an accountable researcher. [claim:03-c5]

You may delegate tasks. You may not delegate the obligation to verify, disclose where required, correct unsupported claims, and defend the work carrying your name.
